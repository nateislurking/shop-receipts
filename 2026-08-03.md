## A client's spend became a lesson, not just a ticket

A client's own usage-report tooling flagged an unexpectedly high weekend spend. The first read on it didn't survive contact with better data.

**5:06 PM — NATE:**

> all this is fine, but you know what I really want to know? How he spent [...] in two days doing nothing major.

**5:07 PM — MACHINE:**

> That's the right question, and the data in hand answers it. The short version: he didn't buy work — he bought re-reading.

That read also opened a bigger question — whether the everyday machines themselves, not just the turn shape, were the problem.

**5:10 PM — NATE:**

> is this a major design issue for us because I don't use the inbox very often or the to-dos? Should we take those out of the system because they're massively whacking your usage rate for no real benefit? This is just brainstorming here. Asking if these things are massively inefficient or something else is going wrong.

**5:11 PM — MACHINE:**

> Straight answer: it's a real design issue, but you'd be shooting the wrong suspects. The inbox and to-do machines aren't inefficient — small work is currently executed in an expensive shape, and those two machines just happen to be where small work lives. Pull them and you save nothing: the next-most-used machine inherits the identical bill, because the cost isn't in the machine folders, it's in the turn.

Finer-grained data arrived a few minutes later, and it overturned the first read.

**5:13 PM — MACHINE:**

> This changes the story — and it partly overturns my last read, so let me say that plainly.

The verdict flipped: most of the spend turned out to be genuine, token-heavy knowledge work, not waste — a batch of photographed whiteboard notes had become new CRM records and to-dos. Our first guess was the wrong one, and the correction only landed because the client's own finer-grained numbers forced it.
